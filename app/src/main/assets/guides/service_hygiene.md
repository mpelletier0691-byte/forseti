# Service and Subpoena Hygiene

Service rules are the most common source of avoidable dismissal. The good news:
the rules are mechanical. Follow them and you are fine.

## Service of the summons (Rule 4)

- The plaintiff is responsible for serving the summons and complaint.
- You generally have **90 days** from filing to complete service (Rule 4(m)),
  unless the court extends it for good cause.
- Individuals can be served personally, by leaving copies at their dwelling
  with a person of suitable age and discretion, or by delivering to an
  authorized agent (Rule 4(e)).
- Always file *proof of service* (Rule 4(l)) — usually an affidavit from the
  process server.

## Service of papers after the complaint (Rule 5)

- After the complaint, most papers can be served by mail, by leaving them with
  the clerk if no address is known, or electronically if the recipient
  consented in writing.
- Add three days to your response deadline when you serve by mail or electronic
  means (Rule 6(d)).

## Subpoenas (Rule 45)

- Use a subpoena to demand documents or testimony from a *non-party*. For a
  party, use Rule 33/34 discovery instead.
- Issue subpoenas from the court where the underlying action is pending.
- Provide reasonable advance notice and pay statutory witness fees if you
  command attendance.
- Service must be made personally; first-class mail is not enough.

## Common pitfalls

- Serving the wrong entity (e.g. a subsidiary instead of the registered agent
  for the parent corporation).
- Letting the 90-day clock expire without filing for an extension.
- Forgetting the certificate of service on every filing other than the initial
  complaint.
