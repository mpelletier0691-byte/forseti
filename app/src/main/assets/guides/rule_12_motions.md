# Rule 12 Motions, Briefly

Rule 12 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure groups together the early
defenses you can raise *before* you file an answer. Knowing the menu is half the
battle.

## The defenses you can move on

Under Rule 12(b), seven specific defenses can be raised by motion instead of in
your answer:

1. Lack of subject-matter jurisdiction
2. Lack of personal jurisdiction
3. Improper venue
4. Insufficient process
5. Insufficient service of process
6. Failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted
7. Failure to join a party under Rule 19

You may also seek a *more definite statement* under Rule 12(e) when a complaint
is so vague or ambiguous that you cannot reasonably prepare a response, and you
may move to *strike* under Rule 12(f).

## Why timing matters

Filing a Rule 12 motion changes the clock. Your answer deadline is generally
suspended until 14 days after the court rules on the motion (Rule 12(a)(4)).
That cuts both ways — it buys you breathing room, but it also means the case
sits without an answer of record until the motion is decided.

## Common misuse

The motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim (Rule 12(b)(6)) is *not* a
mini-trial. Courts test only the legal sufficiency of the complaint, accepting
the well-pleaded factual allegations as true. If you find yourself wanting to
argue *facts*, that is summary judgment territory, not Rule 12.
