# 10 Mistakes That Get Pro Se Cases Dismissed

Avoiding any one of these gives you better odds than half the pro se litigants in the country.

## 1. Missing the 90-day service window (Rule 4(m))

If a defendant is not served within 90 days of filing, the court **must** either dismiss the claim against that defendant or order service within a specified time. Calendar this immediately after filing.

## 2. Filing a complaint that "lumps" defendants

Each defendant must know what *they* personally did. "Defendants conspired to..." without naming who-did-what often gets dismissed under Rule 8 / Twombly-Iqbal.

## 3. Skipping the Rule 7.1 disclosure statement

Required from any nongovernmental corporate party and from any party (including individuals) in a diversity action. Failing to file invites Rule 11 trouble.

## 4. Ignoring discovery requests

Even if every interrogatory is bad, you must respond - even if the response is **objection**. Silence becomes admission under Rule 36, and Rule 37 sanctions can include dismissal.

## 5. Using your complaint as evidence at summary judgment

A complaint is a pleading, not evidence. At Rule 56 you need affidavits, declarations, deposition transcripts, or authenticated documents.

## 6. Missing the 28-day Rule 59 / Rule 50(b) deadlines

These post-trial deadlines are **jurisdictional** in many circuits. The court has no power to extend them. File on day 27 if you must.

## 7. Filing the wrong kind of motion to reconsider

After judgment, **Rule 59(e)** (28 days) and **Rule 60(b)** (1 year for grounds 1-3, otherwise reasonable time) are mutually exclusive. Pick the right one or your motion gets denied as untimely.

## 8. Forgetting the certificate of service

Every paper served on a party requires proof. Most local rules require a Certificate of Service appended to the filing itself. No certificate, no service.

## 9. Conflating courts

Federal civil procedure is governed by FRCP. State courts have their **own** rules of civil procedure. Filing the wrong rule citation in front of a state judge undermines your credibility.

## 10. Not appearing

The single most expensive mistake. If you cannot be there, **file a motion to continue** the moment you know - never just no-show.
